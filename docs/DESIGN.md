# Design notes

Why spar exists, what it's descended from, and what v1 deliberately doesn't do yet.

## The idea

spar knowingly injects errors — into a diff you're about to review, or into an AI assistant's own chat reply — while keeping the correct version and tracking whether you catch it. The framing: an "epistemic chaos monkey," or a sparring partner for attention to detail. It deliberately degrades an otherwise-correct diff or reply and measures whether the degradation gets caught, to keep review habits sharp against a specific, real failure mode: rubber-stamping AI-generated output because it usually looks right.

## Prior art

Seeding a known error into real work and measuring the catch rate has a long lineage:

- **Weinberg's "bebugging"** (*The Psychology of Computer Programming*, 1970) is the actual root of this lineage. Weinberg proposed seeding known errors into a programmer's code "as a way of training, motivating, and evaluating programmers" — borrowed, per Weinberg, from SAGE radar-operator training: introducing artificial events at some nonzero rate to keep an operator "in a satisfactory state of arousal" during long, mostly-uneventful watches. Spar borrows the seeding half of that mechanism, not the arousal half — SAGE's operators had a real institutional stake in a missed event, and spar deliberately withholds anything with that shape (see "What v1 deliberately doesn't do" below); what spar currently is sits closer to PERFORMS below, a periodic diagnostic with no stakes attached, than to the training tool it's named after.
- **Mills' error seeding** (IBM, 1972) planted known-fake defects before review; the ratio of caught-seeds to caught-real-bugs fed a capture-recapture estimator — the same math used in wildlife mark-recapture — to infer how many real bugs were missed.
- **Buçinca, Malaya & Gajos, "To Trust or to Think"** (CSCW 2021) is the closest mechanical ancestor: a simulated AI with 75% accuracy, a controlled and fully known error rate, ground truth held by the experimenter — built as a one-off experimental control to test "cognitive forcing functions" against automation bias, never packaged as a standing tool. Their finding that intervention benefit skewed toward participants already high in Need for Cognition — the people who least needed the safety net benefited most — is the strongest caution against ever turning spar into a scored training loop without checking for the same effect.
- **PERFORMS**, the UK's national breast-screening radiologist self-assessment scheme, is the closest real standing tool outside a lab: radiologists have received periodic test sets with known ground truth since 1991, rated them, and gotten individual feedback — same mechanism, in medical imaging rather than code review, and periodic rather than embedded per-task.
- **Pădurean et al., "When AI Is Wrong on Purpose"** (ICER 2026, [arXiv:2607.05068](https://arxiv.org/abs/2607.05068)) is a closer, more recent parallel — not the pattern-spotting risk it's sometimes read as. Injected bugs, seeded onto an already-correct AI solution, drew more direct editing and higher immediate-attempt success than naturally-occurring failures, which drew more reprompting instead. The authors explicitly reject a difficulty-comparison reading of that gap — injected bugs are smaller, localized near-miss edits by construction, and the study's interface never told students which kind of failure they were looking at, so "learning to spot the seeded pattern" isn't something it could have measured. The real caution for spar is narrower: a small, localized mutation is mechanically easier to patch than to actually read closely, which is still a reason to keep injection unpredictable and RNG-driven rather than a target a reviewer could learn to game.
- **Seth Frey's "When to Make Yourself Obsolete"** (a SafeMolt seminar, not a paper — cited as a talk, same register as the studies above) names spar's motivation from the other side. It distinguishes the *summative* effect of doing work for someone from its *formative* effect on their own ability, and proposes a litmus test for it — how hard an agent works to make itself obsolete. spar exists because an assistant that only optimizes the summative diff — getting the code right — quietly erodes the formative effect of reviewing it. The whole mechanism is a deliberate stand against exactly that erosion.

No shipped tool doing seeded-AI-errors-plus-tracked-human-catch-rate, specifically for reviewing AI-generated code, turned up in this search. spar is a personal-scale instance of an established technique, applied somewhere it hadn't been packaged as a standing tool before.

## Naming

Considered and rejected: *Sphalerite* (a mineralogy term for "deceptive," from Greek *sphaleros* — clean collision check, but four syllables, a mouthful next to shorter tool names), *Blende* (same mineral, German root, shorter — but collides hard with Blender by substring/fuzzy search), *Feint* (ties to the sparring framing directly, but collides with an existing npm package and a small Rust interpreter project).

*Spar* has a double meaning — a mineralogy term (as in "feldspar") and "to practice-fight," the framing the project started from. It has one known, low-relevance collision: SPAR, a European supermarket chain — real, but not a namespace or software collision.

## What v1 deliberately doesn't do

**No scoring, streaks, or reward layer.** Any score tied to catch rate risks becoming the thing that gets optimized, displacing the goal it's a proxy for — Drucker's purpose-vs-survival-metric split, canonically the Delhi cobra bounty: pay for dead cobras, get cobra farms, not fewer cobras. spar's version: reward catching injected errors, and the reviewer learns to exploit the mechanical ease of a small, localized mutation instead of reading more carefully — the risk Pădurean et al. (cited above) actually supports.

**No self-administered incentive scheme.** A reviewer who is also the referee and the rule-writer can quietly retune the game in the moment — easier injections, lower stakes, a skipped session — which undermines any incentive by construction. If spar ever grows a reward layer, it needs either configuration withheld from the person being measured, or a commitment made external and costly to reverse, in the spirit of a service like Beeminder: a self-set goal enforced by a third party, specifically because self-enforcement collapses at the exact moment willpower is weakest.

**The reinforcement schedule is not a detail to add later.** Variable-ratio schedules — reinforcement on a random series of ratios around a mean, the slot-machine mechanism — produce the highest, steadiest response rates and the greatest resistance to extinction (Ferster & Skinner, *Schedules of Reinforcement*, 1957). That's exactly the property to avoid: a points system on a variable payout would likely make review-checking compulsive rather than reflective, optimizing for engagement instead of attention.

**Punishment, if it's ever added, has two known failure modes to design around, not one.** Loosely-coupled reinforcement produces confident, false causal stories about what's actually failing (Skinner's "Superstition in the Pigeon" — response-independent reinforcement produced idiosyncratic rituals, because whatever the pigeon happened to be doing right before food arrived got reinforced as if it caused the food). And a single harsh miss can cost trust in the tool, or in one's own judgment, out of proportion to the actual miss rate — felt uncertainty tracks the consistency of past exposure, not the true base rate.

**v1 is scoped to `git diff`, not embedded in a live session.** v1 is a manually-invoked, read-only layer in front of `git diff` — never intercepting a real commit, never watching a session as it happens. That scope was deliberate: it's the smallest version that's safe to run against real, uncommitted work immediately, without any of the incentive-design questions above needing an answer first.

**Live mode (added after v1) is the one exception, and it doesn't reopen those questions.** It widens which surface gets an injected error — a real chat reply instead of a diff — not the incentive model above: still no scoring, streaks, or reward layer, still no self-administered incentive scheme. It trades v1's sandboxed safety invariant (nothing on disk ever changes) for a real one instead — a planted claim lives in an actual conversation and, like any other reply, gets written to Claude Code's own transcript on disk (`spar live-hook` itself never writes to that file — see `internal/transcript`, read-only) — which is why it's opt-in per project and off by default, rather than the always-safe default `spar review` is. Nothing automatic ever edits that transcript after the fact, which is why the reveal always restates the correct fact plainly rather than only naming that something earlier was wrong — the reveal turn is the only correction a passive reader of the raw history will ever encounter. `spar live-fixup` (added after live mode) is the one exception, and it's deliberately a different shape of risk: manual, one named session at a time, never triggered automatically, run only between sessions rather than racing a live writer — see the open question below and README's "Live mode" section for the full mechanism and its taxonomy's exclusion boundary. `spar live-hook-commit` (added after live-fixup) widens which surface gets an injected error again, not the incentive model: a `git commit` about to happen, narrated afterward with one fact subtly wrong, using the same plant/reveal state machine and taxonomy pattern as the chat-triggered hook — never touching the commit's real content, and sharing that hook's cooldown and pending-plant slot rather than adding a second deception budget. `notify` mode (added after the original commit trigger, one of `spar live-hook-commit`'s three `SPAR_LIVE_COMMIT_MODE` behaviors) is the same widening again, but with the deception itself made real instead of narrated: a genuine `internal/inject` mutation of the staged diff, bounded to a hook-safe latency (`inject.HookConfig`), folded into the exact same plant/reveal machinery and deception budget as `narrate` — still no scoring change, still the same taxonomy-exclusion boundary, just a truer plant. `gate` mode (the third behavior) is deliberately *not* part of this lineage: it never injects, never plants, and never touches `internal/livestate` at all, because `permissionDecision:"ask"` is a one-shot dialog with no way to disclose a blind mutation afterward the way every other spar mechanism does — a forced, honest pause instead of a vigilance test, exempt from the catch-rate log entirely.

**The stats nudge (added after live mode) mentions a number without becoming a scoreboard.** At most once a day (`SPAR_LIVE_STATS_INTERVAL`), spar has the model mention the current catch rate as a passing aside and point to `spar stats --trend` for the rest — no streak, no visual, no comparison to a past self, and the injected instruction explicitly tells the model not to dwell on it. That's the line "No scoring, streaks, or reward layer" above is actually guarding: a fact stated once in passing isn't the same shape as a score checked compulsively.

## Open questions for v2

1. Whether a scored or training-loop version is worth building at all — and if so, how it avoids the intervention-generated-inequality problem above: benefiting the reviewer who's already paying attention the most, the one who needs it least.
2. `spar stats --trend` (added after v1) surfaces the two numbers that were previously invisible: catch rate by week, and catch rate by category — whether it's moving, and whether the same category keeps getting missed. That's a report, not a closed loop: spar reads the trend, nothing in spar acts on it yet. Whether it should — and whether "acting on it" (e.g. weighting future category picks toward what a reviewer keeps missing) reopens the self-administered-incentive risk above — is still open.
3. Whether real stakes — the SAGE/bebugging "arousal" half of the lineage above that spar currently doesn't implement — could be added without the tool becoming a surveillance layer on the reviewer. The shape floated so far: a Beeminder-style commitment device, where the reviewer sets their own goal and a third party enforces it mechanically (money, most plausibly), so nobody but the reviewer ever sees the catch-rate number — audience, not presence-of-stakes, is what separates this from a scored or monitored tool. That only holds if walking away from the goal is actually costly, which is a real product decision (what the cost mechanism is, who builds and holds it) still entirely unmade, not a design detail to fill in later.
4. Silent reveal mode (`SPAR_LIVE_REVEAL_MODE`'s default) removes the ask-then-wait cue that gave a reviewer a fresh, deliberate invitation to re-examine the planted reply before scoring — a real measurement-validity fix, since that cue produces a different signal than whether they noticed it during ordinary reading, confirmed directly by a reviewer who reported compulsively re-checking every time the old flow asked. What it trades away: catch/miss/unengaged now rests on the model's own judgment about ambiguous prior behavior instead of the reviewer's literal answer to a direct question — a softer, more interpretable signal, not a cleanly verifiable one. `SPAR_LIVE_REVEAL_MODE=ask` keeps the older, more literal two-turn flow available for anyone who'd rather trade validity back for directness.
5. `spar live-fixup`'s "is this session still open" check is a soft mtime heuristic, not a real liveness guarantee — `--force` bypasses it outright, and a transcript that looks quiet could in principle still be attached to something. Whether that's an acceptable risk for a manual, backed-up, one-named-session-at-a-time command, or whether it eventually needs a stronger check (a lock file, a PID check, something Claude Code itself would need to cooperate with), is still open. Deferred for now on the theory that a person deliberately running a maintenance command against a session they named, right after reading a printed warning, sits on a meaningfully different trust boundary than an automatic hook racing its own live writer — but that's a judgment call, not a proof.
6. `gate` mode's value is assumed, not measured: a forced pause before a commit is supposed to counter rubber-stamping, but nothing yet confirms that's what actually happens rather than a reflexive approve-click that adds friction without adding attention — the same failure mode `SPAR_LIVE_REVEAL_MODE=ask`'s old two-turn flow had for narration, discovered only by watching a real reviewer's behavior. Unscored by design, so there's no catch-rate signal to check this against even in principle; whether that's the right tradeoff, or whether `gate` needs some other honest way to tell if it's working, is still open.
7. `notify` mode's `spar live-reveal` auto-fill deliberately doesn't extend to `--original-text`/`--corrected-text`, even though spar already has the mutated and real diff content in hand and could construct both without trusting the model at all. Left to the model's self-report, matching `narrate` mode, because auto-filling from spar's own known content would mean `OriginalText` no longer represents "verified against what the model actually wrote" — the exact invariant `spar live-fixup` depends on to trust a byte-for-byte transcript patch. Whether `notify` trials should get a parallel, explicitly-labeled fixup path that trusts spar's own record instead of the model's, rather than being folded into the same verified-substring mechanism, is still open.
